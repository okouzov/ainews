South Korea's $880 Billion AI and Chip Plan Runs Into a Power Wall

29.06.2026 tomshardware.com

President Lee Jae-myung's decade-long "Three Mega Projects" plan pours money into semiconductors, AI data centers and robotics — but the flagship chip megacluster alone could need a quarter of Seoul's entire power supply.

South Korean President Lee Jae-myung unveiled a ten-year, roughly ₩1,350 trillion ($880 billion) public-private investment plan on June 29, branding it the "Three Mega Projects": a coordinated buildout of semiconductor manufacturing, AI data centers and physical AI, or robotics.

The bulk of the money is corporate capital being organized rather than direct state spending. Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix are expected to invest roughly 800 trillion won (about $518 billion) to build new chip production hubs in South Korea's southwest, while another 550 trillion won (about $550 billion) is earmarked for AI data centers from companies including Naver, targeting 8.4 gigawatts of data-center capacity by 2029. The plan also aims to grow the country's humanoid robot market share from 1% to 20% by 2028.

The scale of the ambition runs directly into a physical constraint: electricity. The centerpiece of the chip buildout, the Yongin Semiconductor National Industrial Complex in Gyeonggi Province — the planned Samsung and SK Hynix megacluster — is estimated to require 15 to 16 gigawatts of power at full operation, or roughly a quarter of the entire Seoul metropolitan area's power demand. Local supply currently sits at only about 1.9 gigawatts.

As of a January briefing cited in reporting, roughly 6 gigawatts of the complex's projected 15-gigawatt need had no finalized supply plan. Samsung reportedly needs 9 gigawatts and has secured 6; SK Hynix needs 6 gigawatts and has secured 3 — leaving a substantial gap between what the fabs will require and what the grid can currently promise.

Water presents a parallel problem. A single large memory-chip fab can consume upwards of 100,000 tons of water per day, and the fully built-out Yongin complex is projected to need around 800,000 tons daily. The government's phased solution calls for roughly 200,000 tons per day starting around 2031, drawn from surplus at the Paldang Dam and treated wastewater, scaling up through new intake facilities and pipelines to reach 600,000 tons per day by 2034 — years after some fab capacity is expected to come online.

The plan's backers frame it as existential for South Korea's position in the AI race: at roughly 5% of the country's 2024 economic output, the commitment lands alongside the United States' CHIPS Act spending and China's own reported $295 billion AI infrastructure buildout, each betting that whoever controls the most compute capacity controls the frontier.

For now, the gap between South Korea's financial commitments and its physical infrastructure — power plants, transmission lines, water pipelines — remains the plan's clearest vulnerability, with officials effectively racing to build the grid and the water supply years after committing capital to the chip fabs meant to run on them.
