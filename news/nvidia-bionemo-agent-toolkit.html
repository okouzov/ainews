NVIDIA Launches BioNeMo Agent Toolkit to Give AI Agents Lab Skills

23.06.2026 nvidianews.nvidia.com

A decade of NVIDIA's life-sciences software becomes agent-callable tools for protein design, molecular docking and genomic analysis — with Anthropic, OpenAI and more than 50 partner organizations already building on it.

NVIDIA announced the BioNeMo Agent Toolkit on June 23, 2026, packaging more than a decade of its life-sciences software into a set of tools that AI agents can call directly, aiming to move scientific AI from answering questions to actually running experiments.

The toolkit combines several existing NVIDIA components — BioNeMo, the open Nemotron model family, NemoClaw blueprints, NIM microservices, the OpenShell runtime, the Parabricks genomics platform and the NeMo RL library — into a single agent-callable interface. Rather than requiring a researcher to manually operate each specialized tool, an AI agent can invoke them directly to run protein structure prediction, molecular docking, generative chemistry, genomic analysis, protein design and biomarker discovery workflows.

NVIDIA frames the split of labor plainly through founder and CEO Jensen Huang: "Frontier models are the brains. BioNeMo is the scientific toolbox. Together, they give AI agents the skills of a PhD research assistant and the speed of a supercomputer."

The toolkit already has broad backing. Frontier AI labs building scientific agents — including Anthropic, OpenAI, Edison Scientific, Lila Sciences and Owkin — are integrating with it. So are data and compute platforms such as Benchling, Databricks, Snowflake, Seqera and Certara; pharmaceutical and diagnostics companies including Eli Lilly and Natera; AI-native biology startups such as Boltz, Basecamp Research, Chai Discovery, Dyno, PerturbAI and Proxima; computational drug-design software makers Dassault Systèmes, Cadence's OpenEye and Schrödinger; lab automation vendors Automata, HighRes, Tecan, Thermo Fisher and Medra; infrastructure providers Baseten, Modal and Nebius; and academic labs including the University of Washington's Institute for Protein Design and the Arc Institute.

Early results point to concrete performance gains rather than just integration breadth. The University of Washington's Institute for Protein Design reported achieving twice the performance of its prior setup running RosettaFold3 on BioNeMo's accelerated runtimes — a substantial speedup for a widely used protein-structure prediction tool.

The toolkit is available immediately through NVIDIA's developer resources and on GitHub, positioning it as an open layer that any lab or startup building scientific agents can build on top of, rather than a closed system tied to a single model provider.

The launch reflects a broader shift already underway across NVIDIA's life-sciences push: as frontier labs race to make their models better reasoners, NVIDIA is betting that the harder, more durable value sits in giving those models the specialized instruments — genomic pipelines, chemistry simulators, lab protocols — needed to turn reasoning into actual scientific discovery.
